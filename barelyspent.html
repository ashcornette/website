Overview

Spending trackers want to be your financial advisor. They connect to your bank, categorize every transaction into buckets you'll never check, set budgets you'll ignore by the 15th, graph trends that look impressive in screenshots but mean nothing on a Tuesday when you just want to know if you can afford lunch.

Most people don't need a financial advisor. They need a mirror. "How much did I spend today?" That's the question. One number answers it.

The problem wasn't tracking spending. It was making spending visible without making it complicated.
Goal

Design, build, and ship a spending tracker that shows one number. Tap to add an expense. See the total go up. That's the entire interaction. Prove that awareness is enough.

The Constraint

No categories. No budgets. No bank connections. No graphs. No monthly summaries. Just today's number.

Three layers, each one barely enough:

  • One number. Today's total, front and center. Tap to add. Number goes up. That's it.
  • Keypad. Custom keypad with large touch targets. Tap an amount, tap add. Under 3 seconds.
  • This week. Seven days of history. Swipe to see yesterday. Everything older fades away.
What Shipped

One number

Open the app. See today's total. Tap the plus button to add an expense. Custom keypad with large buttons, inline validation, per-entry cap of $99,999.99. Tap add. Number animates up. Done.

Tap to delete

Made a mistake? Tap the amount in today's ledger. Swipe to delete. No confirmation for small amounts. Over $500 gets a dialog. The ledger is a list, not a database.

Bokashi themes

Same Bokashi colour gradients as Notes. Same time-aware shifting. Same app icon switching. You know these apps are siblings the moment you open one after the other.

Three layouts

Three layouts: portrait, sideways with the phone rotated, and the iPhone Duo book layout where the Duo's two-page spread puts today's ledger on the left and the keypad on the right, like an open chequebook.

Widgets

Home screen widget shows today's total with animated number transitions. Glance at your home screen to see where you're at. No need to open the app.

Lessons

Categories were the first thing cut

Tested with categories. Users spent more time asking "is coffee food or entertainment?" than tracking anything. Removing the question made the app 3 seconds faster per entry, which turned out to be the difference between using it and not.

If the keypad feels right, the app feels right.

The keypad is the product

Most of the design time went into the keypad. Button sizes, spacing, the add animation, the per-entry cap, inline validation. Everything else is secondary.

Awareness changes behaviour

Three testers reported spending less after a week. Not because the app told them to. Because seeing the number made them pause before the second coffee. The intervention is the visibility, not a budget.

One number. That's enough.
